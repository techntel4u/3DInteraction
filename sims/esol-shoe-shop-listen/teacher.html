Teacher notes

This is a pilot, built from Jenny's Entry 3 role-play prep ("Returning a pair of boots") and the Ascentis Entry 3 Speaking and Listening mark scheme. Part 1 covers one exam skill: listening for information, writing it down, and asking the assessor to repeat, slow down or spell. Parts 2 to 4 (answering in the right tense, asking questions, and a full mock) will follow.

How a run works

  1. The start screen sets the task, lists the exam must-dos and lets learners choose where to write: on screen, or on paper (closer to the exam).
  2. Jenny explains the boots can't be exchanged today and says "You might want to write this down". A tap question checks that learners recognise this as the cue to take notes.
  3. Jenny gives three pieces of information from set A, B or C. Each run uses the next set. She says each one once, and her words stay hidden. There is no replay button. To hear it again, learners have to ask her, as in the exam.
  4. Learners ask by speaking, typing or with word tiles. Jenny does what they asked: she spells it, repeats it, says it slowly, or checks what they have written.
  5. On screen, each note is checked when the learner taps ✓ Check. On paper, learners tap "I've written it", compare with the answer and mark their own.
  6. A checklist at the top tracks the exam must-dos. The results sheet lists what they wrote, the correct notes and every question they asked. Print it for a partner check.

Names are not spelled the first time, as in Jenny's video, so learners have to ask. Jenny says "oh" for zero and "double five" in set C, two things learners often miss. Phone numbers are in Ofcom's fictional 01632 960 range, and Brookes is an invented shop.

Notes accept small differences that don't matter: capital or small letters, spaces, a missing title (Mr, Ms), "www." left off a website. Notes with a clear error get a specific tip: the letter O written for zero, "double five" written as one 5, "at" or "dot" written as words in an email address.

Sorry? and Pardon? work: Jenny repeats, and the attempt counts as asking for repetition. They don't count as one of the two correct question forms for criterion 4.4, and learners get a tip to practise a full question too.

How the feedback helps

Feedback on questions follows the same research on corrective feedback as At the doctor's: learners are prompted to fix the error themselves before they are given the correct form. Everything is spoken, and the question is shown in coloured parts: ❓ question word (amber), 👤 who (blue), ⚡ action (teal), 📦 what (green), ➕ please (grey).

  1. First try: Jenny echoes the error ("You can spell that?") and gives a short clue ("A question starts with Can").
  2. Second try: the parts appear with a hint for the missing one, and Jenny recasts ("Can I spell it? Yes, of course").
  3. Third try: Jenny builds the question from the end ("please... that, please... spell that, please... Can you spell that, please?") while the parts light up.
  4. Fourth try: Jenny models the question and then does what the learner wanted, so nobody gets stuck.

For notes, the first prompt sends learners back to Jenny ("Ask Jenny to spell it"). The second shows which characters are right and which need fixing. The third shows the answer to copy.

Speaking: how it works

Learners tap 🎤, ask their question and tap again. On Chrome or Edge (Android, Windows, Mac) and Safari (iPhone, iPad) the browser's own speech recognition is used. On other iPhone browsers the simulation recognises speech on the device, which needs a one-off download of about 50 MB, so start on Wi-Fi. No recordings are sent anywhere or stored. Speech recognition tends to tidy up grammar, so it can hear a correct question when the learner's wasn't. If it gets it wrong, learners tap "That's not what I said". For close accuracy work, use Type or Tiles.

Using it in class

  • Run it on paper mode to match the exam, then use the printed results sheet for the partner check and class discussion, as with your role-play videos.
  • Ask learners to finish each run with all three checklist items ticked. A learner who wrote everything without asking Jenny anything still needs to practise asking.
  • Each run moves to the next set, so learners can do A, B and C in one lesson and compare scores.
  • On a projector, run it as a whole class: learners write on mini whiteboards, and one learner asks Jenny for the class.

Questions to ask the class

  • What did Jenny say before the information? Why is that important in the exam?
  • Which is more polite: What? or Sorry, can you repeat that, please? Why?
  • Why is You can spell that? wrong as a question? Where does can go?
  • How do you say 0 in a phone number? What does "double five" mean?

Notes on the scenario

Jenny the cashier, Brookes and the staff names are fictional. Jenny's voice uses recorded narration where available and the browser's British English voice for anything not yet recorded.
