Keeping people safe

  • Staff: syringe shields, gloves and protective clothing, keep handling time short, keep their distance, and wear personal dosemeters.
  • Patients: every scan must be justified; drink plenty and empty the bladder often to clear tracer; avoid long close contact with young children and pregnant women for the rest of the day (some hospitals say 24 hours – follow local advice).
  • The gamma camera and PET ring give out no radiation – they only detect it.

What you'll learn

Learning outcomes – by the end, you can…

  1. Explain why gamma imaging shows how organs are working: the radiation comes from a tracer inside the patient, not from the machine.
  2. Explain why technetium-99m suits gamma camera imaging (gamma rays only, 140 keV, half-life about 6 hours) and use half-life to work out how much activity is left.
  3. Describe how a gamma camera forms an image: lead collimator, sodium iodide crystal, photomultiplier tubes and computer.
  4. Explain PET: positron emission, annihilation, two 511 keV gamma rays at 180°, coincidence detection, and why it is combined with CT.
  5. Describe how patients and staff are protected, and compare doses with X-ray and CT.

Common misconceptions

✗ The gamma camera fires radiation through the patient, like an X-ray machine.
✓ The camera only detects. The gamma rays come from the tracer inside the patient.
✗ A hot spot on a bone scan means cancer.
✓ A hot spot means increased bone activity. It could be a healing fracture, infection, arthritis or cancer, so other tests are needed.
✗ The tracer stays radioactive in your body for years.
✓ Tc-99m has a half-life of about 6 hours and is also passed out in urine. Hospitals say it is essentially gone within about a day.
✗ A PET scanner detects the positrons.
✓ Positrons travel less than a millimetre before annihilating. PET detects the pair of 511 keV gamma rays they produce.
✗ The collimator focuses gamma rays like a lens.
✓ Gamma rays can’t be focused. The lead collimator blocks every gamma ray that isn’t travelling straight along its holes.

Note on the specification: B3 gives iodine-123 and “technetium-99” as examples of positron-emitting tracers. In fact the medical isotope is technetium-99m, and both Tc-99m and I-123 give out gamma rays directly and are imaged with a gamma camera. PET uses positron emitters such as fluorine-18. This simulation teaches the correct physics – check with your teacher how to word this in assignments.
