1. In gamma imaging, where does the radiation that makes the image come from?
  1. The gamma camera fires it through the patient
  2. A radioactive tracer inside the patient
  3. An X-ray tube under the table
  4. The lead collimator
2. Why is technetium-99m a good tracer for a gamma camera?
  1. It gives out alpha particles, which are easy to detect
  2. It gives out only gamma rays (140 keV) and has a half-life of about 6 hours
  3. Its half-life is several years, so it lasts
  4. It is not radioactive
3. What does the lead collimator do?
  1. Turns gamma rays into light
  2. Amplifies the electrical signal
  3. Only lets through gamma rays travelling straight towards the camera, so the image is sharp
  4. Protects the patient from radiation given out by the camera
4. A bone scan shows a hot spot in a rib. What does it tell the doctor?
  1. The rib definitely has cancer
  2. Increased bone activity there – it could be a fracture, infection, arthritis or cancer, so more tests are needed
  3. The rib has no blood supply
  4. The camera is faulty
5. What does a PET scanner actually detect?
  1. Positrons leaving the body
  2. X-rays from a rotating tube
  3. Pairs of 511 keV gamma rays travelling in opposite directions at the same time
  4. Gamma rays from technetium-99m
6. A dose of technetium-99m has an activity of 400 MBq at 9 am. Ignoring removal by the body, what is its activity at about 9 pm?
  1. 200 MBq
  2. 100 MBq
  3. 50 MBq
  4. 0 MBq
