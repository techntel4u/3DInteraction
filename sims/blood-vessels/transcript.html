Guided tour

1. A closed loop

Blood travels in a closed loop of vessels. Arteries carry blood away from the heart. They branch into arterioles, then into capillaries inside the tissues. Capillaries join into venules, and venules join into veins, which carry blood back to the heart. The heart is a simple shape here. You explored its inside in Part 1.

2. Arteries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure. Their walls are thick, with a strong middle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibres. The wall stretches with each heartbeat and springs back, which you can feel as a pulse. The lumen, the space inside, is narrow compared with the wall. Most arteries carry oxygenated blood, but the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.

3. Arterioles

Arteries branch into smaller arterioles. Their walls are mostly smooth muscle. When this muscle contracts, the arteriole narrows. This is vasoconstriction, and less blood flows into the capillaries beyond it. When the muscle relaxes, the arteriole widens. This is vasodilation, and more blood flows through. Blood pressure drops as blood passes through the arterioles.

4. Capillaries

Capillaries are the smallest vessels. Their wall is only one cell thick, and red blood cells pass through in single file, slowly. This gives a short distance and plenty of time for exchange. Oxygen and nutrients such as glucose pass out to the muscle cells. Carbon dioxide and lactate pass in, to be carried away. The red blood cells stay inside the capillary.

5. Venules

Capillaries join together to form venules. Venules collect deoxygenated blood, which now carries carbon dioxide and lactate, and join together to form veins. Blood pressure here is low, and the blood flows smoothly with no pulse.

6. Veins and valves

Veins carry blood back towards the heart at low pressure. Their walls are thinner, with less muscle, and the lumen is wide. Veins have valves that only open towards the heart and shut to stop blood flowing backwards. When the skeletal muscles around a vein contract, they squeeze it and push blood towards the heart. This is the skeletal muscle pump. Watch which valve opens with each squeeze.

7. What blood is made of

An adult has about 5 litres of blood. When a sample is spun in a tube, it separates. Plasma, the straw-coloured liquid at the top, is about 55%. Red blood cells, at the bottom, are about 45%. White blood cells and platelets make a thin pale layer in between, less than 1%.

8. Red blood cells and plasma

Red blood cells carry oxygen. Each one is a disc that is thinner in the middle, which gives a large surface area, and it has no nucleus, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. Oxygen joins with haemoglobin in the lungs and is released in the tissues. Red cells also carry some carbon dioxide. Plasma is mostly water. It carries dissolved nutrients such as glucose, carbon dioxide, hormones and heat around the body.

9. White blood cells and platelets

White blood cells fight infection. Some engulf and digest germs such as bacteria, and others make antibodies. They are larger than red cells and have a nucleus. Platelets are tiny fragments of cells. When a blood vessel is cut, platelets stick to the damaged wall and to each other to form a plug, and they help the blood to clot. This stops the bleeding.

10. The vascular shunt

During exercise the working muscles need far more oxygen. Arterioles supplying the muscles vasodilate, and arterioles supplying organs such as the stomach and gut vasoconstrict. Blood is redirected to where it is needed. This is the vascular shunt. At rest the muscles get about 20% of the blood pumped out by the heart. In hard exercise this can rise to about 80%, and the heart pumps out much more blood each minute too.

11. Controlling body temperature

Blood vessels also help control body temperature. When you get hot, for example during exercise, arterioles supplying the skin vasodilate. More warm blood flows near the surface and heat is lost to the air. In the cold, skin arterioles vasoconstrict, so less blood flows near the surface and heat is kept in the core. The model now shows a cold day. Switch Body to Exercise to compare.

12. Follow a red blood cell

Now follow one red blood cell round the loop. Say each stop out loud before the cell reaches it: heart, artery, arteriole, capillary in the working muscle, venule, vein, and back to the heart. Watch its colour change in the capillary as it gives up oxygen.

Common mix-ups

1. Mix-up: Blood in veins is blue.

Right idea: Deoxygenated blood is dark red. Blue is only a colour code in diagrams. Veins can look blue through the skin.

2. Mix-up: Arteries always carry oxygenated blood.

Right idea: Arteries are named by direction: they carry blood away from the heart. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.

3. Mix-up: Red blood cells leave the capillaries to reach the muscles.

Right idea: Red cells stay inside. Oxygen and nutrients cross the thin wall into the muscle cells, and carbon dioxide and lactate cross back in.

4. Mix-up: Vasoconstriction happens in the heart.

Right idea: It happens in blood vessels, mainly arterioles, when the smooth muscle in their walls contracts and narrows them.

5. Mix-up: Red blood cells fight infection.

Right idea: White blood cells fight infection. Red blood cells carry oxygen.
