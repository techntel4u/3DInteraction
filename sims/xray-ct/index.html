What you'll learn

Learning outcomes – by the end, you can…

  1. Explain how an X-ray tube produces X-rays: a heated cathode filament emits electrons, a high voltage accelerates them, and they strike a tungsten anode.
  2. Explain why bone looks white, soft tissue grey and air-filled lung dark on an X-ray image, using the idea of attenuation.
  3. Describe how a CT scanner measures attenuation from many angles as the tube rotates and the table moves, and how a computer reconstructs slices (tomograms) that stack into a 3D view.
  4. Use CT numbers (Hounsfield units) to match regions of a CT image to air/lung, fat, soft tissue and bone.
  5. Explain the risks of ionising radiation and how hospitals protect patients and staff: justification, keeping doses as low as reasonably practicable, time, distance, shielding and personal dosemeters.

Common misconceptions

✗ X-rays bounce off bones to make the picture.
✓ The image is a “shadow”: bone absorbs more X-rays, so fewer reach the detector behind it.
✗ White on an X-ray image is where the X-rays hit.
✓ White is where few X-rays reached the detector. Dark areas (lungs, air) are where most got through.
✗ A CT scan is just one big X-ray photo.
✓ CT takes measurements from many angles as the tube rotates; a computer reconstructs each cross-sectional slice.
✗ After an X-ray or CT scan, you are radioactive.
✓ X-rays stop the moment the tube switches off. Nothing radioactive is left in the body.
✗ A CT scan gives about the same dose as a chest X-ray.
✓ A typical chest CT (6.6 mSv) is roughly 470 times a chest X-ray (0.014 mSv), so every scan must be justified.
